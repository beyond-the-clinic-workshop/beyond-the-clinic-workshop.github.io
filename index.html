Beyond the Clinic

AI for Patient-Driven Health

AI models for personal health could bring continuous, individualized care to the large share of health decisions made outside the clinic. The question is no longer whether AI will shape how people manage their health, but whether it will do so well.

About

Most health decisions happen outside the clinic

Sensors that continuously measure human physiology have become cheap enough to wear every day, and AI models are increasingly capable of interpreting the data they collect. Foundation models pretrained on physiological data from millions of individuals learn representations that transfer across tasks and devices, and language-model agents can interpret personal health records, answer health questions, support behavior change, and hold diagnostic and disease-management conversations.

Turning these advances into better health outcomes requires more than accurate predictions. Personal health data are often incomplete, fragmented across devices, and collected without standardized protocols or reliable labels. A glucose alert may change what someone eats, and a sleep recommendation may change how they exercise, so interventions change the very data models learn from. And safety depends not only on model accuracy but on how people interpret and act on AI recommendations over repeated interactions.

Our mission is to bring together the research communities developing the methods needed for safe and effective patient-driven health AI. Progress requires advances in machine learning, causal inference, and human–AI interaction, informed by clinical expertise and the experiences of patients themselves. This workshop provides a forum for discussion across these disciplines, focused on three research directions.

Three research directions

1

Learning from heterogeneous personal health data

Models that integrate wearables, laboratory results, clinical records, and self-reported data, and combine population knowledge with an individual’s own history.

2

Adaptive interventions and sequential decision-making

Methods that personalize when and how to intervene, accounting for how each intervention changes behavior, physiology, and the data that follow.

3

Human–AI interaction, evaluation, and safety

How people understand and act on AI outputs, and evaluation that extends from model responses to decisions and health outcomes.

Call for papers

Research directions

We invite papers across all three research directions. Key topics include, but are not limited to:

01

Learning from heterogeneous personal health data

Personal health records combine wearables, laboratory results, clinical records, and self-reported information, collected at different times and without standardized protocols. How can models integrate these sources and combine knowledge learned across populations with evidence accumulated by an individual?

  • Representation learning: Foundation models and self-supervised methods for longitudinal, multimodal personal health data, including learning with limited labels.
  • Heterogeneous data integration: Methods for combining wearable signals, medical records, laboratory measurements, and self-reported data across different temporal resolutions.
  • Learning from imperfect observations: Modeling informative missingness, selective recording, measurement noise, and changes in devices and sensors.
  • Individualization and generalization: Combining population-level evidence with individual health histories; adapting models across patients, devices, and populations while accounting for uncertainty and bias.
  • Learning without reliable ground truth: Evaluating personalized predictions with sparse labels, and using longitudinal observations to generate and test hypotheses about human physiology.
02

Adaptive interventions and sequential decision-making

Unlike predictions, interventions change behavior and physiology, and with them the data on which subsequent decisions are based. How can systems estimate the effects of interventions, adapt to individuals over time, and keep learning safe and effective after deployment?

  • Adaptive intervention policies: Reinforcement learning, contextual bandits, and other methods for personalizing the timing, content, and frequency of health interventions.
  • Causal evaluation: Micro-randomized trials, N-of-1 experiments, off-policy evaluation, and counterfactual methods for estimating individual intervention effects.
  • Learning under feedback: Methods that account for how recommendations change behavior, physiological measurements, and the data available for subsequent learning.
  • Continual personalization: Online learning and adaptation as an individual’s health, behavior, and response to interventions change over time.
  • Long-term and safe optimization: Decision-making with delayed or imperfect outcomes, limited opportunities for exploration, and constraints on potentially harmful interventions.
03

Human–AI interaction, evaluation, and safety

The effectiveness of personal health AI depends on how individuals understand and act on its outputs. How should systems communicate uncertainty and support decisions without routine clinical supervision, and how can evaluation extend to the decisions people make with AI and their subsequent health outcomes?

  • Uncertainty and information seeking: Identifying missing information, communicating uncertainty, abstaining when appropriate, and escalating decisions that require clinical input.
  • Human–AI evaluation: Interactive benchmarks, longitudinal user studies, and prospective trials that assess joint decision-making and downstream health outcomes.
  • Robustness and safety: Detecting and mitigating harmful advice, misleading premises, sycophancy, human misuse and abuse of AI models, and inappropriate reliance on model outputs.
  • Responsible deployment: Privacy-preserving methods, equitable performance across patient populations, and continuous evaluation and monitoring after deployment.

We particularly encourage applications in women’s health, mental health, chronic condition management, and the health of underserved populations.

Submission tracks

Up to 8 pages

Regular papers

Original methodological research, empirical studies, benchmarks, and position papers relevant to patient-driven health AI.

Up to 2 pages

Tiny papers

Early-stage research, preliminary findings, negative results, reanalyses, and new research perspectives that would benefit from community feedback.

Designed to lower the barrier for authors outside the machine learning conference circuit, including clinicians, patient researchers, and digital health researchers.

  • Page limits exclude references. Submissions use the ICLR format and undergo double-blind review on OpenReview (link to be announced).
  • Selected papers receive spotlight presentations and spotlight paper awards, announced in the closing session. All accepted papers are presented in the poster sessions.
  • The workshop is non-archival, so authors can publish their work elsewhere later. Accepted papers will be publicly available on OpenReview.
  • Papers already published at other machine learning conferences, including the ICLR main track, will not be accepted.
  • We follow the ICLR 2027 policy on AI usage. AI tools may assist with research and writing, subject to disclosure requirements, but AI systems will not serve as primary authors or reviewers. AI-generated submissions are not accepted in the tiny papers track.

Program

An interactive day

Patient-driven health spans communities that rarely share a venue: machine learning researchers, clinicians, industry scientists, and patients. The program is built around exchange rather than one-way presentation, with more time for panels, roundtables, and a debate (140 minutes) than for invited talks (110 minutes, including Q&A).

Expert panels

The morning panel pairs an industry AI leader, a physician-informatician, and a patient advocate to discuss how each envisions AI supporting health decisions outside the clinic. The afternoon panel brings together researchers translating wearable AI into consumer products and elite sport. Both reserve time for audience questions.

Interactive roundtables

Attendees, invited speakers, panelists, and organizers meet in small groups under the Chatham House Rule. Tables have no fixed topics, so participants raise the questions they find most pressing. Ideas from each table feed into the afternoon debate and panel and are synthesized in the closing remarks.

Live-vote debate

Are wearable data clinically useful? An industry machine learning scientist and a clinician-researcher argue opposing positions. Live vote counts stay on screen throughout, so the room’s reaction to each argument is visible in real time, followed by open Q&A.

Spotlights and posters

Contributed work is featured through spotlight presentations, with awards for spotlight papers, and two poster sessions that give authors extended time for discussion.
